## Facts and hazards that matter for these two languages (research and cite each; mark "needs a native check" when unsure — do not guess)

**Polish**
- Diacritics `ą ć ę ł ń ó ś ź ż`. **`ł`/`Ł` does not decompose under Unicode NFD**, so an accent-folding function built on `unicodedata.normalize("NFD")` leaves it unchanged. Check how `fold_accents` works today and give Polish an explicit map (`ł→l`) without changing any other language's result. Prove it with tests (`łódź`, `Łukasz`, `wąż`, `żółć`).
- Seven cases: names and nouns inflect (`Marek` → `Marka`, `do Marka`, `z Markiem`). Matching names between a subtitle and the transcript, and keeping a name unchanged in Arabic, must not depend on the nominative form. Say what the tokenizer should and should not try to do (no stemming claims you cannot prove).
- No articles; subject pronouns are often dropped (the verb carries the person), so a line may have no pronoun at all.
- **Gender is visible in the source.** Past-tense verbs and many adjectives mark the speaker's and the addressee's gender (`zrobiłem`/`zrobiłam`, `byłeś`/`byłaś`, `poszedł`/`poszła`), and plural has a virile/non-virile split (`oni`/`one`, `poszli`/`poszły`). This is very useful for Arabic, which marks gender too. Describe rules the translator prompt and `gender_context` can rely on, with the exceptions (the polite `pan/pani/państwo` forms take third-person verbs; a female speaker can use a masculine noun for her job).
- Address: `ty` / `wy` (informal), `pan` / `pani` / `państwo` (formal, third person). Say how each should map to Arabic and what must be kept in the translation.
- Abbreviations that end in a full stop without ending a sentence: `p.` (pan/pani), `dr`, `prof.`, `inż.`, `mgr`, `ks.`, `ul.`, `godz.`, `np.`, `itd.`, `itp.`, `tzn.`, `tj.`, `m.in.`, `ok.`, `r.` after a year (`w 2026 r.`), `w.` (century), `ww.`, `por.`, `zob.`, `tys.`, `zł`. Verify each against a cited source (the Polish language council, the PWN dictionary, the official spelling rules) and mark which are only subtitle-practice candidates. Ordinal numbers are written `1.`, `2.` (like German) and dates `5 maja 2026 r.`: be conservative and document the ambiguity.
- Quotes `„ ”` (low-high) and `« »`; dialogue dash is usually an en or em dash with a space; subtitles often use a hyphen. Decimal comma (`3,5`), thousands separator a space or a dot.
- Sound descriptions in subtitles: `[muzyka]`, `(wzdycha)`, `(śmiech)`, `[hałas]`, `[oklaski]` — candidates, mark for native check. Cite a published subtitle style guide for the language if one exists and say which version you read.

**Dutch**
- `ij` is one letter in the alphabet (`IJsselmeer` is capitalised as a unit). Long compounds are written as one word (`bedrijfsaansprakelijkheidsverzekering`). Document how matching and splitting treat them (no compound splitting is expected; say so).
- Clitics and elisions: `'t`, `'s` (`'s avonds`), `'n`, `m'n`, `z'n`, `d'r`, `zo'n`, `ie`; apostrophes are straight and typographic. Decide tokenisation like the French/Italian elisions, with the original text preserved.
- Abbreviations: `dhr.`, `mw.`, `mevr.`, `mr.` (a lawyer, not Mister), `drs.`, `dr.`, `ir.`, `prof.`, `bijv.`, `enz.`, `o.a.`, `m.b.t.`, `d.w.z.`, `t.o.v.`, `a.u.b.`, `nr.`, `blz.`, `m.i.`, `bv.` — verify against the Dutch Language Union (Taalunie) rules / the official Woordenlijst and mark candidates. Note `etc.`.
- Address: `u` (formal) / `je`, `jij` (informal), plus Flemish `gij`/`ge`. Keep one `nl` entry (Netherlands and Belgium) as decided for Spanish and Portuguese; `variants` stays empty. Say what is lost.
- Gender: Dutch marks gender only in third-person pronouns (`hij`/`zij`/`ze`) and some job titles; verbs and adjectives do not tell the speaker's gender. Gender for Arabic therefore depends on names, titles, context and the voice, as it does for English. Say so plainly.
- Quotes `„ ”` (traditional), `“ ”` and `‘ ’` (modern), `« »`; decimal comma, dot or space for thousands; ordinals `1e`, `2e`, `1ste`, `8ste`.
- Word order: the verb goes to the end of subordinate clauses and separable verbs split (`ik bel je morgen op`), so a line cut in the wrong place changes the meaning. Say what the line splitter can safely rely on.
- False friends and idioms that the translator must get right: `half drie` = 2:30 (not 3:30), `kwart over` / `kwart voor`, `eventueel` = possibly, `actueel` = current, `brutaal` = cheeky, `gezellig`, `mooi` (nice/beautiful), `lekker`. Mark each expected Arabic form that a native must confirm.
- Sound descriptions: `[muziek]`, `(zucht)`, `(lacht)`, `[geluid]`, `[applaus]` — candidates, native check. Cite the published Dutch subtitle style guide you read and its version.

**Both**
- The Arabic target: keep MSA rules unchanged. Names stay as names (transliterate, never translate); numbers, dates and units are kept; titles are kept.
- The 40 function words per language are **curated**, not frequency-ranked (as for the six): say so, never label them "most frequent". Make sure the lists separate Dutch from English and German, and Polish from the other Latin-script languages (these are the realistic confusions); add a confusion test (see Part D).

## One more fact to build around (do not try to fix it in this job)

The cloned voice's native Arabic accent is currently a best-effort result for non-English source languages: the voice provider documents its accent-adaptation step as available only for voices whose original audio was English, and a voice cloned from another language matches the speaker best in that language. Lisan AI tries the step for every language and falls back to the plain copied voice when it fails. This is Claude's and Ali's concern, not a code change in this job. **What it means for you:** in every copy draft, help text and gold-set note, do not claim a "native accent" for Dutch or Polish dubs, and do not write anything that contradicts the new help entry "Will the Arabic voice sound like a native Arabic speaker if my video is not in English?" (see `help.html`). Mention in the handoff if any measurement or source you find says otherwise.
